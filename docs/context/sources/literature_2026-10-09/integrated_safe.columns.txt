We follow a similar approach: consider a multi-metric framework
that integrates four dimensions: sustainability, accuracy, fairness and
explainability into a single metric. And, similarly as in Nguyen and

The advantage of our proposal is a precise determination of the
relative importance of each dimension, as they are all measured with
the same metric. A possible disadvantage is that we are not using
existing metrics but, rather, new ones, which need to be understood,
especially in comparison with the standard ones.

2.1. Rank Graduation Accuracy (RGA)
Accuracy reflects how closely the output of an AI system aligns with
the expected or true values. For instance, in the NIST Risk Management
Framework (National Institute for Standards and Technology, 2023),
accuracy is described as the degree to which results or estimates
approximate actual or accepted reference values.
In both statistics and machine learning, accuracy is traditionally
measured using well-established metrics. For continuous outcomes, the
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is commonly used, while for binary
classification tasks, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Character-
istic Curve (AUROC) serves as a standard performance indicator.
To address the limitations of task-specific accuracy metrics, a more
general, response-agnostic measure was proposed—Rank Graduation
Accuracy (RGA). An important property is that the RGA measure is
equal to the AUROC when applied to binary outcomes. Also, it extends
naturally to ordinal and continuous targets, preserving a consistent
interpretation across different problem types.

We propose a decomposition of the RGA metric into a vector of
partial RGA values, each corresponding to a segment of the ranked
predictions. This approach goes beyond the use of a single summary
score and provides a more detailed view of how different parts of the
data contribute to the overall accuracy of the model.
This decomposition is particularly important in the context of the
unified compliance score, where we simulate cyber-attacks that selec-
tively damage or degrade parts of the data. In such scenarios, relying
solely on the final RGA score is insufficient, as it may hide local weak-
nesses or failure points. By breaking the RGA into partial contributions,
we can assess how specific segments of the model’s ranking respond to
such disruptions. This allows for a more robust evaluation of the model
accuracy under adverse conditions.

2.2. Rank Graduation Robustness (RGR)
Robustness in machine learning generally refers to a model’s ability
to maintain stable performance under perturbations or unexpected
conditions. The robustness literature addresses various dimensions in-
cluding adversarial attacks, natural corruptions, distribution shifts, and
algorithmic stability (Cohen et al., 2019; Hendrycks & Dietterich, 2019;
National Institute for Standards and Technology, 2025; Rousseeuw &
Leroy, 1987). In this work, we focus on adversarial attacks by measur-
ing how model performance degrades under systematic perturbations
to assess stability across different architectures.
Most robustness metrics quantify change in accuracy or rankings
after perturbation. The S.A.F.E-AI framework introduces Rank Gradua-
tion Robustness (RGR), which, for each feature, compares the ranks of
predictions on original versus perturbed input.

as Explainable AI (XAI), has become significant for building trust,

ensuring safety and enabling meaningful oversight. Traditional ‘‘white-

interpret, using clear mathematical relationships and well-understood
statistical methods. In contrast, modern high-capacity models, such as
random forests and deep neural networks, are often seen as ’black

Explainability is usually defined as the ability to represent how an
AI system operates in a form that is understandable to humans (Na-

enabling user monitoring, intervention, and correction of AI system
behavior.

on Shapley values (Guidotti et al., 2018). However, Shapley-based
methods are computationally demanding and become impractical when
dealing with a large number of input variables. The Rank Graduation
Explainability metric (RGE) proposed in Babaei et al. (2025) overcomes
this drawback.

3. Data
To illustrate our methodology, we utilize data from the Home
Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA), the most comprehensive publicly
available dataset on the U.S. mortgage market. Enacted in 1975 and
implemented through Regulation C, HMDA requires various financial
institutions to report detailed information on individual mortgage ap-
plications. This data serves multiple critical functions: HMDA helps
assess whether lenders are meeting community credit needs, helps
policy makers develop equitable housing initiatives, and highlights
potential patterns of discriminatory lending. To ensure the borrower’s
privacy, the publicly released data is anonymized. We examine two dif-
ferent subsets of HMDA data for the loan applications filed in 2017: the
New York dataset which consists of 446902 loans, and the California
dataset containing 1714459 loans.
After filtering out irrelevant and non-informative features and re-
ducing the number of categories in some variables, we focus on 21
features for further analysis, as described in Table 1.
For comparison we also apply the proposal to a synthetic dataset,
which contains 3000 employee records with eight features including
salary, age, education, job tenure, previous experience, minority status
and gender. The binary target variable follows an XOR (exclusive OR)
pattern based on age and salary, where the positive class is assigned
when exactly one of two conditions holds: either an employee is young
(below median age) with high salary (above median), or older (above
median age) with low salary (below median). This XOR relationship
creates a non-linear decision boundary that cannot be captured by any
single hyperplane, as the two target classes occupy diagonally opposite
quadrants in the age-salary feature space. Consequently, linear models
such as logistic regression are fundamentally incapable of learning this
pattern without feature transformations or interactions. This design
makes this dataset suitable for detecting meaningful variations in our
metrics that might be obscured in datasets where all models perform
similarly.
4. Empirical findings
This section presents a comparative analysis of models performance
across our datasets using AURGA, AURGR, and AURGE metrics. We
evaluate AURGR under two perturbation strategies (Swapping and
Gaussian noise), compare RGE results with Shapley values and demon-

A diverse set of machine learning models was applied. These models
are used as tools to generate predictions upon which the new scoring
methodology is evaluated. The selection includes models from different
algorithmic families: logistic, tree-based, ensemble and neural network
approaches, ensuring a well-rounded and representative set of models.

We demonstrated that model selection for compliance scoring appli-
cations requires a nuanced, multi-faceted evaluation that accounts for
both absolute performance and relative stability. The comparative anal-
ysis across multiple datasets and evaluation methodologies reveals that
compliance performance fundamentally depends on perturbation types.
Linear models exhibit resilience to Gaussian noise but are more vulner-
able to Swapping perturbations, whereas tree-based models display the
opposite trend. This perturbation-dependent behavior indicates that no
single architecture achieves universal superiority across all compliance
dimensions; thus, the optimal model choice must consider the dominant
data quality degradation modes in the target deployment environment.
The proposed Compliance Score framework, which integrates mul-
tiple aggregation methods (Arithmetic Mean, Geometric Mean, RMS,
and TOPSIS), offers an evaluation tool adaptable to diverse orga-
nizational priorities through weighted schemes. The strong correla-
tion between RGE scores and Shapley values supports the validity
of the perturbation-based explainability approach, although observed
discrepancies for lower-ranked features suggest the need for further
investigation.
From an ethical and regulatory viewpoint, the unified metric pro-
vides a clear tool that can be employed by auditors and regulators to
assess the compliance of any AI application, and to highlight where
that compliance is to be improved (e.g. in security, accuracy, explain-
ability, and also in fairness, considering the explainability of protected
variables). The same auditors and regulators could also give different
weights to the four dimensions, in line with the evolving policies
and their implementations, as described for example, in European
Commission, 2023; National Institute for Standards and Technology,
2023).
Future research should address the limitations of the present work.
First of all, our proposal should be compared with methods that
integrate individual existing metrics, such as Nguyen and Do (2025), in
real case scenarios, as we did for the credit data in this paper. Second,
the proposal should be extended beyond tabular data, to consider, for
example, image processing and natural language processing and large
language models, as in Liang et al. (2022).
Another line of research that could address the limitations of the
current work should concern investigation into how the Compliance
Score framework can be adapted to meet evolving regulatory require-
ments, including the integration of fairness metrics beyond the pre-
sented measures, represents another critical research direction. This
includes exploring how the framework can accommodate emerging AI
governance standards and audit requirements in financial services.
Further research should focus on the challenge of reconciling con-
flicting model recommendations when different perturbation scenarios
favor different architectures. Dynamic weighting schemes that adapt
to specific regulatory requirements or business contexts represent a
promising direction, potentially incorporating stakeholder preferences
through interactive multi-criteria decision-making approaches. Estab-
lishing standardized benchmarking protocols and extending the frame-
work to continuous outcome variables would broaden its applicability.
